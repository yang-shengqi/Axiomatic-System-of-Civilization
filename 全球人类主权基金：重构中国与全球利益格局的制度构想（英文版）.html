Introduction: Why Discuss This Plan?

For thousands of years, human society has been entangled in a recurring predicament: the accumulation of wealth inevitably leads to its concentration; concentration leads to the monopolization of power; monopolization solidifies into unfair distribution; unfair distribution accumulates class hatred; and the eruption of hatred destroys the existing order. After the old order collapses, a new one is built on the ruins, and the cycle begins anew.

This cycle runs through all of recorded human history. It disregards ideology, cultural tradition, or geographic boundaries. It asks only one question: To whom does the ownership of public wealth ultimately belong?

To date, humanity has offered only two answers: either it belongs to a "divinely ordained" monarch, or to an "abstract" people—and the latter, in practice, invariably alienates into the former: nominal ownership by all, yet actual control by the few.

This plan proposes a third answer: public wealth belongs to no one; it can only be returned to everyone.

The method of return is not a one‑time distribution, not egalitarian leveling, not violent expropriation, but an irreversible institutional design—rendering wealth legally, physically, and governancially detached from any individual, family, political party, or administrative body, becoming an inalienable legal right of every citizen from birth.

Four Fundamental Needs

Sense of Security
Is the survival baseline institutionally guaranteed, rather than dependent on anyone's charity or market fluctuations?
Sense of Gain
Do the fruits of labor and creation truly belong to oneself, rather than being siphoned off by an invisible distribution mechanism?
Sense of Happiness
Beyond survival and security, is there still room to pursue a life worth living?
Sense of Participation
Is one recognized as a full member of the community, rather than excluded from the circle of public wealth beneficiaries?

The poor of this country, the poor of the Earth—they are not rhetorical objects, not charity cases, not voting chips. They are the masters of this plan.

To ensure that the poorest child in the last village on Earth, from the moment of birth, possesses a survival guarantee just as inalienable as the richest person on the planet.

Vision

If one day, humanity looks back at this moment, they will find that the nightmares that have haunted us for millennia—war, poverty, disease—are not invincible destinies.

War stems from contention. When everyone's survival no longer depends on anyone's grace, when plunder can secure no extra guarantee, the logic of weapons will dry up at its root.

Poverty stems from scarcity. When public wealth is returned to its creators, when every life from birth possesses an inalienable foundation for survival, "scarcity" is exposed as a lie.

Disease—the most stubborn pain of biological instinct—still requires medicine to conquer. But at least, humanity will no longer lose the chance for treatment due to poverty, no longer overdraw their bodies under survival pressure, no longer abandon hope because of despair.

These three ancient enemies will not retreat amid cheers. They will only, one day, be calmly written into history textbooks by future generations—as evidence that humanity once lived in scarcity and fear, not as a permanent destiny.

Origin Context: A Local Cross‑Section

The theoretical starting point of this plan is China—an economy with a massive stock of state‑owned capital, a deep tradition of administrative monopolies, and intense tension in wealth distribution.

In China's reality, the accumulation and distribution of national wealth present a profound paradox: on one hand, the state owns the world's largest operational state‑owned assets and generates enormous excess profits from monopolistic industries such as tobacco, oil, telecommunications, and finance. On the other hand, the wealth Gini coefficient—a measure of wealth inequality—has long remained above 0.6‑0.7 (with 0.4 generally considered the warning line for high inequality). The wealthiest 10% of the population control about 68% of society's wealth, while the bottom 50% often hold less than 5% of total wealth.

This is not a problem unique to China. Any country with large‑scale administrative monopolies and state‑owned capital faces the same structural dilemma: when the ownership of public wealth heavily overlaps with private power, "public ownership" effectively alienates into "official ownership" at the operational level.

It is in questioning this problem that the core logic of this plan emerges: if an institutional arrangement exists that can take the excess profits generated by monopolistic industries—morally belonging to all citizens—and inject them into a fund beyond administrative control through independent legislation, complete divestiture, physical isolation, and perpetual lock‑in, then the deadlock of wealth distribution can be untied at its source.

China thus becomes the earliest laboratory of ideas for this plan. Yet once completed, these designs prove equally applicable to any country with similar power structures. This plan starts from this local cross‑section, but ultimately points to a common human proposition that transcends any national border.

The Starting Point of the Problem: Six Ruptures

Rupture One: Between Ownership and Sense of Gain

China possesses the world's largest monopolistic state‑owned assets—China Tobacco's annual tax and profit exceeds 1.6 trillion RMB, and profits from oil, electricity, telecommunications, and other industries are equally enormous. However, under the current system, most of these enormous profits flow directly into the national treasury for annual budget expenditures. Ordinary citizens have little intuitive perception of this.

Rupture Two: Between Inside and Outside the System

Civil servants have annuities, stable expectations, and systemic protection. Private sector employees, gig workers, and the unemployed do not. Society faces the anxiety of "getting old before getting rich" and the fairness anxiety caused by the welfare gap. This is not a Gini coefficient problem—it is a deep‑seated tear of "you and I are not in the same system."

Rupture Three: The Rupture of Trust Between China and the World

"Decoupling" has become the global narrative. The outside world does not believe that Chinese capital can flow in and out freely without arbitrary intervention. China does not fully trust external rules. Both sides spin their wheels in suspicion.

Rupture Four: The Rupture of the Time Dimension – Short‑Termism

Any leadership under any system has a "short‑term performance bias"—they want to see results within their term. Five or ten years is already considered long‑term. Thirty years? That's for the next few terms. This is not a moral issue; it is an issue of incentive structure.

Rupture Five: The Generational Rupture

Young people see the "pension gap," "delayed retirement," and the "35‑year‑old crisis." After rational calculation, they conclude: "I won't live to see the day I get the money, or the money I get won't be worth anything." Over 40 million people have stopped paying into the system—not because they are stupid, but because they are too rational.

Rupture Six: The Universality of Human Short‑Sightedness

This is not China's problem—it is all of humanity's problem. All people—fund managers, officials, ordinary citizens—have a "present bias": current benefits outweigh future benefits. This is code written into our brains by evolution.

Three institutional ruptures + two time‑dimensional ruptures + one fundamental human rupture = the complete set of problems that institutional design must address.

Chapter One · General Principles

1.1 Core Insight: Wealth is Not Inherently Scarce

The underlying assumption of all conflicts in human society is that "survival resources are naturally scarce, hence they must be contested." But this assumption itself needs to be examined.

A modern state's accumulated public wealth—including land appreciation gains, natural resource rents, seigniorage from currency issuance, profits of state‑owned enterprises, excess returns from administrative monopolies—far exceeds the amount needed to guarantee the basic survival of all its citizens.

The real scarcity is not a lack of total volume, but the monopolization of the ownership of this public wealth by a very few individuals at the very inception of institutional design.

The core insight of this plan is: if this portion of public wealth, inherently belonging to all citizens, is returned to everyone at its source through an irreversible institutional design, the illusion of scarcity will dissipate at the root. When everyone's basic survival no longer depends on dependence on a particular employer, a particular institution, a particular benefactor, the most fundamental "scrambling" motive in human society will be systematically weakened.

This is not a plan about "how to make society fairer." It is a plan about "how to enable civilization to break out of its death loop."

1.2 Definition of the Plan

The Human Wealth Fund, in its ultimate form, will be the Earth Human Basic Survival Bottom‑Line Trust.

It does not belong to any country, any institution, or any particular group. It belongs only to every specific, irreplaceable life form with equal beneficiary rights. When a newborn baby, whether born in a war‑torn land or a bustling metropolis, possesses a completely equal and inalienable account, only then does this institution truly transcend countries, nations, and ideologies.

In operational terms, it is a permanent institutional arrangement with the excess profits of national administrative monopolistic industries and public accumulation as the initial capital source, independent legislation as legal protection, complete divestiture and physical isolation as governance principles, perpetual lock‑in of initial capital as a safety bottom line, global multi‑asset allocation as a means of value appreciation, an open tiered account system as the beneficiary structure, and with all beneficiary accounts receiving returns in proportion to their contributed capital.

Chapter Two · Institutional Engine

2.1 Why Must It Be Monopolistic Industry Profits?

The sole source of founding capital chosen by this plan is the excess profits of national administrative monopolistic industries. This choice is not made for convenience, but for the sole moral and legal correctness.

Monopolistic profits do not belong to the institutions that operate them.

The decisive factor enabling an industry to generate excess profits far exceeding the level of market competition is not its operational capability, but the exclusive power granted to it by the state in the name of all citizens. All citizens have tacitly consented to the restriction of their consumption choices, to the exclusive occupation of public resources by specific institutions—this series of "tacit consents" constitutes the true source of monopolistic profits.

Injecting monopolistic profits into a universal fund is not taxation, not nationalization, not wealth redistribution. It is restitution—the restitution of a public wealth that has been held in proxy for a long time but has never truly been returned.

2.2 Scale Effect: Too Big to Be Ignored

A "small sum" of universal dividends is politically welfare, economically supplementary, and psychologically a gift. It cannot change the social contract, cannot build a community of shared interests, and cannot stop the inertia of contention.

But the scale of monopolistic industry profits naturally solves this problem. Taking China as an example, in the 2020s, the annual total profit of major monopolistic industries—tobacco, oil, electricity, telecommunications—accounted for approximately 5%‑8% of GDP. If a considerable proportion of these profits is completely divested in one go and injected into the fund, the resulting initial capital pool will be a figure that no political force can ignore.

2.3 Implicit Meaning of the Launch Mechanism

The plan itself does not describe the launcher. It does not define their identity, specify their origin, or presuppose their motivation.

But the essence of the capital injection behavior is this: injecting monopolistic industry profits into the fund is not generous charity, not noble donation, not forward‑looking investment. It is acknowledging a long‑suppressed fact—this wealth never belonged to the launcher.

No head of the executive branch has the authority to modify, freeze, or repeal any provision of the fund's basic law by executive order.

The amendment threshold of constitutional law is extremely high—any attempt at tampering would require triggering a full‑scale constitutional crisis. This cost itself is the most powerful defense mechanism.

Personal Account: Time Rules for Dividends and Principal

Every citizen, from the date of birth, possesses a personal‑level account in the fund. The principal in the account is locked until the beneficiary reaches the age of 60. After reaching age 60, the beneficiary may decide how to dispose of the principal: keep it in the fund to receive dividends, withdraw in annual installments, or arrange for posthumous disposition.

Ten‑Year Deferred Distribution Rule

  • Immediate distribution: 70% of annual dividends are distributed directly to the beneficiary's digital account on a quarterly or monthly basis.
  • Deferred distribution: 30% of annual dividends enter the beneficiary's "deferred account," continuing to be invested and appreciated within the fund. The beneficiary can withdraw it only after a full 10 years.
  • Beneficiaries who have reached age 60, or who are diagnosed with a terminal illness, may apply for early withdrawal of the entire deferred account balance.
