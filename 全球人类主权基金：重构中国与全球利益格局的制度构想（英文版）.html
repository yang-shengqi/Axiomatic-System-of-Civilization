Introduction

For thousands of years, human society has been trapped in a cyclical predicament: the accumulation of wealth inevitably leads to its concentration; the concentration of wealth inevitably leads to the monopolization of power; the monopolization of power inevitably solidifies into distributive injustice; distributive injustice inevitably accumulates class resentment; the eruption of resentment inevitably destroys the existing order. After the old order collapses, a new order is built upon the ruins, and the cycle begins anew.

This cycle runs through all of recorded human history. It asks no questions about ideology, cultural tradition, or geographical boundaries. It asks only one thing: to whom does the ownership of public wealth belong?

To date, humanity has had only two answers to this question: either it belongs to the "divinely ordained" monarch, or it belongs to the "abstract" people—yet the latter, at the operational level, always degenerates into the former: nominal ownership by all, effective control by a few.

This proposal attempts to offer a third answer: public wealth belongs to no one; it can only be returned to everyone. And the means of return is not a one‑time distribution, not leveling by egalitarianism, not expropriation by violent revolution—but an irreversible institutional design that severs wealth, legally, physically, and governancely, from the control of any individual, family, political party, or administrative body, making it an inalienable legal right of all citizens from birth.

This proposal ultimately seeks to answer not economic questions, not political questions, but four fundamental needs of human beings as individuals surviving within national communities:

  • Security—whether the survival floor is institutionally guaranteed, rather than dependent on anyone's benevolence or market fluctuations.
  • Gain—whether the fruits of labor and creation truly belong to oneself, rather than being expropriated by invisible distribution mechanisms.
  • Happiness—whether, beyond survival and security, there remains the margin to pursue a life worth living.
  • Participation—whether one is recognized as a formal member of the community, rather than excluded from the circle of public wealth beneficiaries.

These four needs, in any era and under any system, are humanity's most basic expectations of public order. The entire design of this proposal is to provide an irreversible institutional response to these four expectations.

The poor of this nation, the poor of the earth—they are not rhetorical objects of this proposal, not subjects of charity, not bargaining chips of votes. They are the masters of this proposal. Every word of this proposal is ultimately written for them.

The ambition of this proposal is to ensure that the poorest child in the last village on Earth, from the moment of birth, possesses—just like the richest person on the planet—a survival guarantee that no one can take away. Not because he deserves pity, but because he deserves to be treated as a whole person.

Origin Context: A Local Cross‑Section

The theoretical starting point of this proposal is China—an economy with a vast stock of state‑owned capital, a deep tradition of administrative monopoly, and intense wealth‑differentiation tensions.

In China's reality, the accumulation and distribution of national wealth presents a profound paradox: on one hand, the state possesses the world's largest stock of operating state‑owned assets, with enormous net asset value, and the colossal excess profits generated annually by monopoly industries such as tobacco, oil, telecommunications, and finance. On the other hand, the wealth Gini coefficient—reflecting inequality in wealth distribution—has long remained elevated, with the wealthiest 10% of the population holding approximately 68% of social wealth, while the bottom 50% often possess less than 5% of total social wealth. The concentration of wealth is not a natural outcome of market competition, but a concomitant phenomenon of administrative power's deep involvement in resource allocation and interest distribution.

This is not a problem unique to China. Any country with large‑scale administrative monopoly and state capital faces the same structural dilemma: when the ownership of public wealth heavily overlaps with private power, "public ownership" degenerates at the operational level into "official ownership," and the rights of all citizens as ultimate owners always lack an institutionalized channel of realization.

China thus becomes the earliest thought laboratory for this proposal. Many of the proposal's key technical constraints—such as how to prevent a strong administrative system from re‑establishing de facto control after the fund's establishment, how to ensure that beneficiary rights are not revoked due to citizens' political attitudes—originate from targeted responses to China's governance characteristics. But once these designs are completed, it becomes clear that they apply equally to any country with a similar power structure.

This proposal starts from this local cross‑section, yet ultimately points to a common human proposition that transcends the borders of any single nation.

The Starting Point: Six Fractures

Fracture 1: Between Ownership and the Sense of Gain

China possesses the world's largest stock of monopolistic state‑owned assets—China Tobacco's annual tax and profit exceeds 1.6 trillion yuan, and monopoly profits from oil, electricity, telecommunications, and other industries are equally massive. These wealth fundamentally derives from resources owned by all citizens and market monopoly privileges, and should rightfully belong to all citizens.

However, under the current system, most of these enormous profits go directly into state finances for annual budgetary expenditures. Ordinary citizens lack any direct perception of this, and "public ownership of state assets" remains largely at the conceptual level, failing to translate into visible, tangible, and disposable property income under each person's name.

Fracture 2: Between Insiders and Outsiders of the System

Civil servants have pensions, stable expectations, and institutional protection. Private‑sector employees, flexible workers, and the unemployed do not. Society faces the anxiety of "aging before getting rich," and the fairness anxiety triggered by the welfare gap between insiders and outsiders of the system. This is not a Gini coefficient issue. This is the deep rupture of "you and I are not in the same system."

Fracture 3: The Trust Fracture Between China and the World

"Decoupling" has become a global narrative. The outside world does not believe that Chinese capital can enter and exit freely without arbitrary intervention. China does not fully trust external rules. Both sides spin in mutual suspicion.

Fracture 4: The Time Dimension—Short‑Termism in Decision‑Making

Under any system, leadership has a "short‑term performance preference"—I want to see results within my term. Five or ten years is already considered long‑term; thirty years? That's a matter for the next several administrations. This is not a moral issue; it is an incentive‑structure issue.

Fracture 5: Intergenerational Fracture—Young People Aren't Buying

Young people see "pension gap," "delayed retirement," "the 35‑year‑old crisis," and after rational calculation conclude—"I won't live to the day I receive it, or the money I receive won't be worth anything." Flexible workers pay over a thousand yuan a month, accounting for 1/3 or even 1/2 of their income. Young people turn to more extreme self‑rescue—"FIRE" (Financial Independence, Retire Early), "save enough to live on interest," or simply "lie flat."

Fracture 6: The Universality of Human Short‑Termism

This is not China's problem. This is humanity's problem. Everyone—fund managers, officials, ordinary people—has "present bias": immediate interests weigh more heavily than future interests. This is code written into our brains by evolution.

Three fractures + two time‑dimension fractures + one fundamental human fracture = the complete problem set that institutional design must address.

Chapter II: Institutional Engine—Source and Nature of Founding Capital

2.1 Why Must It Be Monopoly Industry Profits?

The starting point of any institutional design is "where does the money come from." If this question is not answered well, the entire institutional edifice will rot from its foundation.

Monopoly profits do not belong to the institutions that operate them.

The decisive factor for an industry generating excess profits far beyond market competition levels is not its operational capability, but the exclusive powers granted to it by the state in the name of all citizens. These powers include: restrictions on market entry, exclusivity in resource possession, protection of pricing power, and legal barriers to competition.

All citizens have tacitly accepted that their consumption choices are restricted, that public resources are monopolized by specific institutions, and that market competition is distorted by administrative power—this series of "tacit consents" constitutes the true source of monopoly profits.

Injecting monopoly profits into a universal fund is not expropriation, not nationalization, not wealth redistribution. It is restitution—the restitution of a public wealth that has been long held in trust but never truly returned.

2.2 International Legal and Moral Review of This Funding Source

Under international law, the capital injection of this proposal does not constitute "expropriation" or "nationalization" under any definition. The reasons are:

  • First, the operational rights of monopoly industries and the ownership of monopoly profits have always been two separate legal objects in international investment law practice. The state's power to dispose of monopoly industry profits falls within the policy space of sovereign actors and has never been entirely excluded by bilateral investment treaties or international investment arbitration.
  • Second, even if it enters arbitration, the promoting country can clearly argue: this is not depriving investors of their legitimate rights, but making a belated ownership adjustment to a source of income that is naturally public in nature.
  • Third, at the level of global ethical consensus, "monopoly windfall profits returning to society" is a cross‑ideological consensus.

2.3 Scale Effect: Too Big to Ignore

Another key reason for choosing monopoly industry profits as founding capital is scale.

A "small amount" of universal dividend is, politically, welfare; economically, a supplement; psychologically, a favor. It cannot change the social contract, cannot build a community of interests, cannot stop the inertia of competition.

But the scale of monopoly industry profits naturally solves this problem. Globally, the annual profits of administrative monopoly industries in any country account for a non‑negligible share of its economy. Taking China as an example, the combined annual profits of major monopoly industries—tobacco, oil, electricity, telecommunications—account for approximately 5%‑8% of GDP. By completely stripping and injecting a significant proportion of these profits into the fund in one go, the resulting initial capital pool will be a figure that no political force can ignore.

This scale means: for citizens, the income it generates is systemic, enough to change a family's survival floor; for international capital markets, a sovereign fund that starts with enormous monopoly profits and is completely legally independent will become a target for the world's top financial institutions from its inception; for any potential tamperers, when the fund's scale becomes visible and tangible to all citizens, any attempt to touch it is an act against the entire citizenry. Scale itself is the fund's moat.

3.3 Complete Severance

First: Complete severance of property rights—The ownership of assets transferred to the fund is legally and completely transferred to the fund's legal entity. After transfer, the original owner retains no recourse, beneficiary, mortgage, or pledge rights.

Second: Complete severance of personnel rights—No member of the fund's highest decision‑making body may be appointed or nominated by current or former public officials of the founding state, senior party officials, or persons with direct interest in monopoly industries.

Third: Complete severance and solidification of beneficiary rights—The scope of beneficiaries and the rules for receiving returns in proportion to contributed capital must be written into the body of the fund's basic law. No beneficiary may be deprived of beneficiary rights due to political stance, religious belief, or criminal conviction.

3.4 Physical Isolation

Layer 1: Physical isolation of capital from the parent—The fund's legal place of registration must be an international financial center with a highly mature rule of law. All fund assets are custodied with globally top‑tier independent custodian banks. The founding state's government, judiciary, or any individual has no freeze, query, or transfer rights over assets in custodian accounts.

Layer 2: Physical isolation of governance from power—The fund is not subject to the directives of any institution or individual of the founding state. The fund's investment decisions, rebalancing plans, holdings details, and other internal information need not be reported to the founding state's government or head of state. The only recipients of information disclosure are the beneficiaries.

Layer 3: Physical isolation of distribution from identity—The fund's beneficiary distributions do not pass through any administrative intermediaries. Each citizen's digital identity is directly bound to the fund's distribution system through blockchain or equivalent security technologies. Dividends are automatically credited without any government department's approval, allocation, or disbursement.

Complete Severance: Leaves no interface for attacking the fund—no backdoors, no nominal holdings, no residual control rights

Physical Isolation: Renders physical means of attacking the fund ineffective—assets abroad, law abroad, governance abroad

Three locks simultaneously securing the same door. If any single lock is breached, two others remain. The precondition for all three locks to be breached is the simultaneous collapse of domestic political forces, the international legal system, and the global financial order. This level of catastrophe is beyond the scope this proposal needs to guard against.

Every citizen, from the day of birth, possesses an individual account in the fund.   • Immediate receipt portion: 70% of annual dividends, distributed directly to beneficiaries' digital accounts quarterly or monthly.

  • The funds and investment returns in the deferred account are fully owned by the beneficiary.

4.3.5 Age Reset Rule for Intergenerational Inheritance

The second beneficiary or testamentary heir of an individual account inherits the annual dividend beneficiary rights, not the ownership of the account's principal. Before the heir reaches the required age, the account's annual dividends are either defaulted into the fund's public income pool or rolled into the account's principal as specified by the original beneficiary.

4.3.6 Distribution in Proportion to Contribution

The income distribution of all account levels is uniformly executed in proportion to each account's contributed capital in the total capital. The rule is written into the fund's charter, automatically calculated and disbursed by algorithms, with no discretionary power for anyone.

4.3.7 Compliance Review Principles

All new account capital sources must pass the fund's independent compliance committee's anti‑money laundering and counter‑terrorism financing review. The fund does not accept capital from sources that cannot be traced or violate international anti‑money laundering standards.

4.3.9 Prohibition on Beneficiary Rights Transfer

The beneficiary rights of any account at any level may not be transferred, pledged, mortgaged, or traded in secondary markets in any form. The fund does not recognize, execute, or register any form of beneficiary rights transfer.

  • First: Direct binding of pension interests—touching the fund means touching my retirement money.

Throughout human history, truly immortal institutions are not maintained by force, but by making enough people its beneficiaries and guardians.

5.11 The System's Goodwill Back Door

The entirety of this proposal points to one unavoidable premise: the system's launch requires a decision. The proposal itself does not describe the person making this decision. But the system design includes a self‑evident entry point—a goodwill back door.

For anyone or any group in control of a scale of funds sufficient to launch the initial capital, if they fundamentally understand that this wealth does not belong to them and decide to return it in an irreversible way—the entire institutional design of this proposal is prepared for them.

The greatest tragedy of human society over thousands of years is not poverty, not war, not the abuse of power. The greatest tragedy is that public wealth occupied by a few, though it could have been returned, never was.
